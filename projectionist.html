As You Like It (1936)

Directed by: Paul Czinner
Watched: 23 Jun 2026
Rating: ★★★☆☆

It's excellent—especially its screenplay. Fully half the play must be cut, and there's no room at all for pause, but at 96 minutes it feels cinematic and cheerful.

Laurence Olivier is basically a god who walks the Forest of Arden. It's ridiculous, it strains credulity, and yet there is some sense in it, for Elisabeth Bergner (an Austrian Jewish refugee, incidentally, along with director Paul Czinner) outmatches him both in beauty and in screen presence (once she embodies Ganymede, certainly—it's a particularly strong Ganymede performance).

What little slack exists is left for the actors playing Jaques and Frederick to chew on.

Whisper of the Heart (1995)

Directed by: Yoshifumi Kondō
Watched: 11 Jun 2026
Rating: ★★★★☆

So beautiful. In Whisper of the Heart, Shizuku, our protagonist, experiences a bit of a breakthrough. In many ways, reminiscent of Miyazaki's work. However, where Miyazaki would deliver his signature brand of somno-mythic esoterica, Kondō instead kneads in a deep and gentle socio-economic ecology.

A literal and figurative ecology, where relations between people and non-human animals feel inextricably connected to weather, shelter, sunlight, hunger, mineral genesis, and cross-species mutuality.

The Testament of Ann Lee (2026)

Directed by: Mona Fastvold
Watched: 19 May 2026
Rating: ★★★☆☆

Shot on 35mm film by the duo behind The Brutalist, one third of The Testament of Ann Lee is indistinguishable from a neoclassical painting or Dutch still life: unnaturally bright skies, white interiors, dark varnished foreground detail, and wide angle lenses mimicking revolutionary, history-making framing.

These moments become deeply personal through Amanda Seyfried's delivery. She's captivating as Lee. I find it interesting how both her performance and the story itself invoke and tie together proto- and contemporary feminist threads, seeking a balance through tradition and liberation that perhaps we ourselves have yet to canonize.

And yet the story felt somewhat shallow to me, with under-written scenes and dialogue, intrusive voiceover, lacklustre lyrics, and an apparent need to rush through the last ten years of Ann Lee's life instead of climaxing on one of the many triumphant moments we only glimpse.

Gadjo Dilo (1997)

Directed by: Tony Gatlif
Watched: 3 Feb 2026
Rating: ★★★★☆

Gadjo Dilo is the story of a young man from France who goes to Romania looking for something sad and beautiful and, by Jove, he finds it. Lots of music, dancing, tears, and vodka — perfectly viable alternatives when you don't share a language.

Rona Hartner gives a great performance. Tons of knowing, silent nods to the prejudice Romani people are subject to, as well as a few loud ones. This is one of those movies about the joy and sorrow of life, so if you're looking to stand up and feel human after a 102 minute sitting experience you will not be left hanging.

Vagabond (1985)

Directed by: Agnes Varda
Watched: 18 Jul 2025
Rating: ★★★★★

Flawless conceptual execution paired with genius level direction and cinematography, is what this is. Sandrine Bonnaire's performance of Mona is so inviting and deeply felt. There is a supremacy and an innocence holding together the world of Vagabond that evokes equally security and danger. The world is almost fair. Not fair, but candid. The world approaches fairness when its people have room in their hearts to regard and reach out for each other, even if it's just a passing thing.

Memories (1995)

Directed by: Katsuhiro Otomo, Koji Morimoto, Tensai Okamura
Watched: 02 June 2026
Rating: ★★★★☆

Really good. Another anthology, like Neo Tokyo, this time with Katsuhiro Otomo's manga short stories as the texts for adaptation and he himself in the role of executive producer. Otomo also directs the short Cannon Fodder, which is the weakest of the three. Mind you, what constitutes weakness here is still very elevated.

Stink Bomb is funny and beautifully animated, but Magnetic Rose is the real gem here. Magnetic Rose is unmatched in its sub-genre of operatic space horror. Directed by Kōji Morimoto (who also wrote and directed Beyond from The Animatrix, just to add some context), this short shows you the wide scope of an unknown universe only by looking back on very specific heirlooms of humanity's past.

The themes at the heart of the story are right in line with Mizoguchi's Ugetsu, and anyone approaching this collection from an interest in the great ghost stories of Japanese cinema must watch at least this one short.
